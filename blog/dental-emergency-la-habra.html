Dental Emergencies: What to Do Before You Can Be Seen

Not every dental problem can wait for Monday — and a few shouldn't go to a dentist at all. How to tell the difference, and what actually helps in the meantime.

Diamond's Dental Inc. · 331 E La Habra Blvd, La Habra, CA · (562) 448-3018 · Updated 2026-08-18

Short answer: call a dentist the same day for a knocked-out tooth, a broken tooth, swelling of the face or gums, or pain that keeps you awake. Go to an emergency room instead if you have difficulty breathing or swallowing, swelling that is spreading toward the eye or neck, uncontrolled bleeding, or a possible broken jaw. Diamond's Dental Inc. can be reached at (562) 448-3018 during office hours.

Call a dentist promptly

These situations are urgent but are handled at a dental office, not a hospital:

  • A knocked-out permanent tooth. Time matters more here than with almost any other dental problem. Handle the tooth by the crown — the chewing surface — not the root. If it's dirty, rinse gently with milk or saline; don't scrub it. If you can, place it back in the socket and hold it there. If you can't, keep it in milk and get to a dentist immediately.
  • A cracked, broken or badly chipped tooth, especially if it's sensitive to air or temperature.
  • Persistent toothache, particularly throbbing pain that wakes you at night or doesn't respond to over-the-counter pain relief.
  • Swelling in the gum or face, or a pimple-like bump on the gum, which can signal infection.
  • A lost filling or crown, which leaves the tooth structurally exposed.
  • A broken wire or bracket on braces that is cutting into the cheek or lip.

Go to an emergency room

Some symptoms are beyond what a dental office is equipped to treat, and delay is genuinely dangerous. Go to the nearest emergency department — or call 911 — if there is difficulty breathing or swallowing, swelling spreading toward the eye or down the neck, bleeding that won't stop, a suspected broken or dislocated jaw, or a high fever alongside facial swelling.

Dental infections can spread into the tissues of the face and neck. That is rare, but it is a medical emergency, not a dental inconvenience.

What to do while you wait

None of this is a substitute for treatment — it's what helps until you're seen:

  • Rinse gently with warm salt water.
  • Use a cold compress on the outside of the cheek for swelling.
  • Take over-the-counter pain relief as directed on the label, if it's appropriate for you.
  • Keep your head slightly elevated when lying down; throbbing often worsens flat.
  • Avoid chewing on that side, and avoid very hot or very cold foods.

Two things to avoid: don't place aspirin directly against the gum — it can burn the tissue — and don't apply heat to a swollen area, which can encourage infection to spread.

Why weekend pain is so common

Toothaches don't genuinely spike on weekends; the offices do. Pain that builds Thursday gets tolerated through Friday and becomes unbearable Saturday, when most practices in the area are closed. That's a large part of why Diamond's Dental Inc. keeps Saturday hours, 8:00 AM to 3:00 PM — a problem that starts midweek doesn't have to wait until Monday.

If something is hurting on a Thursday, call. Same-day availability is real but limited, and an early call is worth far more than a stoic weekend.

Preventing the emergency you haven't had yet

Most dental emergencies are not accidents — they're long-running problems that finally reached a nerve. A cavity found at a routine exam is a filling; ignored, the same tooth becomes a root canal or an extraction. Regular exams and cleanings are the cheapest, least dramatic version of the same care. If you play contact sports or grind your teeth at night, ask about a guard; both are common causes of fractures we could have prevented.

This guide is general information, not a diagnosis. If you are in pain or unsure how serious a symptom is, call the office at (562) 448-3018 or seek emergency care.

Frequently asked

What counts as a dental emergency?

A knocked-out permanent tooth, a broken or cracked tooth, severe or sleep-disrupting toothache, facial or gum swelling, a lost filling or crown, and braces wires cutting into the mouth all warrant a same-day call to a dentist. Difficulty breathing or swallowing, spreading facial swelling, uncontrolled bleeding or a suspected broken jaw require an emergency room instead.

What should I do if a tooth gets knocked out?

Pick the tooth up by the crown, not the root. Rinse it gently with milk or saline if dirty, without scrubbing. If possible, place it back in the socket and hold it there; otherwise keep it in milk. See a dentist immediately, as the chance of saving the tooth drops quickly with time.

How can I relieve tooth pain before my appointment?

Rinse with warm salt water, apply a cold compress to the outside of the cheek, take over-the-counter pain relief as directed if appropriate for you, and keep your head elevated when lying down. Do not place aspirin directly on the gum and do not apply heat to swelling.

When should I go to the emergency room instead of a dentist?

Go to an emergency room or call 911 for difficulty breathing or swallowing, swelling spreading toward the eye or neck, bleeding that will not stop, a suspected broken or dislocated jaw, or high fever with facial swelling.
